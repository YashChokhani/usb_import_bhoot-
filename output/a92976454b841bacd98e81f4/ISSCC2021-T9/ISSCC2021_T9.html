Slide 13 –Negative and Positive Feedback

Slide 14 –Feedback Stability: Nyquist Stability Criterion
The key concepts for feedback amplifier stability rely on the Nyquist stability criterion that was originally published in 1932 by Bell Labs; unfortunately, I will not be able to cover this in detail, but it is important to acknowledge. A Nyquist plot is obtained by tracing the magnitude and the phase of the loop-gain in the complex plane from DC, along increasing frequency; the theory offers mathematical proof of stability, but for most practical feedback amplifier cases, without right half plane poles, a necessary and sufficient condition for stability is that the Nyquist plot in this complex plane of the loop-gain, does not encircle the real -1 point. This real -1 point is equivalent of 180° phase-shift, and of course, the negative feedback turning positive.

So the loop gain is a function of poles and zeros; the plot on this left shows a stable system, why? Because by the time the phase is inverted, the amplitude is significantly below 1.

The plot here on the right, shows an unstable system; again, by the time the phase is inverted, it still has enough gain so the system can become unstable; these are the main concepts and you can actually revisit the Nyquist stability criteria, for a much more complete proof of stability.

Slide 15 –A practical simplification

As illustrated on the right, the degree of the stability can be estimated by looking at how much attenuation happens at the 180° point; this is called the gain margin, or what the phase is when the amplitude is 1, and this is called the phase-margin.

Slide 16 –Bode Plots: Refresher

Bode plots are a graphical method to represent a small-signal frequency response of a system at a particular operating point. They represent this complex transfer function of the loop-gain, or the amplitude, or the gain in general, by plotting the magnitude typically in dB, and a phase of argument, in degrees.

It’s essential to note that they assume a system that is linearized at a particular operating point, therefore, provide only a snapshot; this is similar to a 2D simulation of a 3D system. It is critical to follow them up with transient simulations and disturbances, especially through startup transients, input and outputs, railing, current limit, etc.

Slide 29 –Amplifiers: Number of stages
So one of the most important choice that you have to make in terms of stability and for your overall system, is the number of gain stages; so let's take a look at the key tradeoffs between these.

Single stage amplifiers are inherently stable; they're simple and power efficient, but they have a limited gain and output impedance; their output range is limited, very sensitive to load conditions and have a limited current drive capability.

Two-stage amplifiers improve on the output impedance and the output range, more insensitive to load; the input range and output range are decoupled and offered by their current drive capability. They still can be reasonably simple, have good load capacitance drive and settling time, and reasonably power efficient.

But in some cases, their gain is still limited; they have higher offset and noise, more distortion, and the current drive capability, especially for bipolar implementation is still limited; so when you need to improve on those, then you go to a 3-stage amplifier. Slide 33 –Types of compensation
We said that by frequency compensation, we want to assure that the negative feedback does not turn into positive; the ways to achieve this is either reducing the loop-gain, the amplitude of the signal that is fed back sufficiently before it is inverted, or to bypass some of the circuit elements, to reduce the overall phase-shift.

Capacitors are elements with impedance reducing over frequencies, and they are also easily integrated; therefore, are one of the most popular choices for frequency compensation. The most commonly applied techniques are load compensation; the load capacitor here provides the dominant compensation, shunt compensation, very large capacitor is used at a high impedance node, to kill off the gain of a particular stage.

A lead compensation also called parallel compensation, adding an additional resistor and capacitor in series into the gain path to cancel out a pole that is introduced by the load. The most common one is Miller compensation; this uses a capacitor across the high gain stage to provide the dominant pole in the frequency response, and this has many variants that we will talk about today, and we can of course, also use feed-forward compensation, using a faster, typically lower gain element, or stage to bypass a high gain stage at particular frequencies.

And there is a whole variety of combination of the above techniques that people have used to achieve the best performance.

We saw that using compensation in negative feedback is very powerful; can it be improved by adding even more gain? Yes, and this concept is called cascode Miller and was patented by Weiser and Reed and also published by Huja.

Adding a common-gate transistor increases the loop-gain and yields a much better load capacitance drive; it also blocks the right half-plane zero that we just discussed and even introduces a left half-plane zero that helps the phase margin even further.

It's important to note that the transconductance of the cascode transistor should be higher than the input transconductance to be effective, but we also need to watch out the additional gain stage we just added, makes the Miller loop itself a two-pole loop.

Basically the output transconductance and the load capacitance form one pole and a cascode transistor in the Miller capacitance, form the second one. When the load capacitance is zero, the dominant pole is formed by the Miller capacitor and things are stable, but when these two poles approach similar frequencies to each other, then of course you get a pretty quick phase drop and you can actually make the amplifier go unstable.

When you further increase the load capacitance, it sets the dominant pole and the middle-loop becomes stable again; so you have to be careful to make sure that you sweep the load capacitance and make sure that for node values, the loop becomes unstable, or at least not for the values that you are designing for.

There is another note of caution and that happens during slewing; you can imagine when all of the current is going through the Miller capacitance, that happens during slewing, now all of a sudden, the current through the MP, and its transconductance drops significantly; when that happens, the gain is dropped in this slewed amp and you can become unstable until the slew condition is stopped.

Slide 47 –Split Cascoded Miller
A way to improve stability when the Miller capacitance and the load capacitance are in a similar order, is to split the compensation between the two techniques we just discussed; so using part of the compensation capacitor as a regular Miller capacitor, and part of it as a Cascode Miller compensation.

The ratio that is typically selected is somewhere between 1 and 6; you can get a better load capacitance drive when the Cascoded Miller is higher and a little bit more even stability with changing load capacitance, when you make the primary Miller compensation larger.

But this key just as mentioned before, it's very important to sweep the load capacitances in small steps and observe small and large signal responses, and pay special attention what happens during slewing.

Slide 48 –“Hybrid” Cascoded Miller
Let's take a look what the transistor level implementation would look like; I'm showing here a folded-cascode amplifier with a rail-to-rail output stage and a class-AB control. The cascode compensation is tied back to the cascode transistors on a P and an N side and the traditional Miller compensation around the output transistors. For better load capacitance drive, you would want to make CM2 larger, but if you run into some stability issues, then you would increase CM1. Generally, best practice for good settling is to make the transconductances of the P and N side transistors about the same, and to keep the two halves of CM2, also to be the same.

As I've mentioned, it is critical to follow up with transient simulations, and sweep output current and load capacitance drive; and it is important to notice that the class-AB control can add an additional delay into the signal path, so you would want to make sure you evaluate both with syncing and sourcing current.

Slide 49 –Pole-Zero Doublets
I warned a couple of times about the existence of pole-zero doublets and I would like to illustrate this a little bit better; I'm showing here plots of the same system amplitude, phase, and step responses, with either perfectly matched pole-zeros shown in red, or when I mismatch them, shown in green and blue. You can tell in the mismatch cases which one happens first looking at the phase plot; when a zero happens first, the phase rises until the pole cancels out its effect conversely in the blue, the phase drops until the zero cancels out its effect. At high frequencies, all of these look the same, but they look very different in the transient step response.

You can see the band match pole-zero doublet settles very quickly, but the mismatches cause a long and so settling which we call settling tails. The lower frequency, this pole-zero doublet mismatches happens, or the larger phase diversion that causes the worse settling problems may be.

Why does this happen? If you just think of in the phase-domain, frequencies reasonably close to each other travel to the system with different delays and this eventually leads to the slow settling.

What can you do about it? Try to do your best to match the pole and zero doublet with normal techniques capacitance and current matching, or try to make sure that where this pole-zero doublet happens, it’s as close to your bandwidth as possible, leading to less settling impact.

Slide 53 –Additional gain stages
As discussed, three stages provide more gain, lower offset, wider input and output ranges, and typically lower distortion; they unfortunately also lead to three high frequency poles that need to be compensated.

In general, for power efficiency, it is worth to explore adding additional gain to existing stages, before adding additional stages; this can of course be difficult, especially for bipolar amplifiers, if rail-to-rail operation is needed to the finite input impedance and their limited current gain.

Slide 54 –Nested Miller Compensation
Nested Miller compensation is the extension of Miller compensation, to three or more stages; the technique is to consider G2 and G3 as an inner-loop; now G1 and CM1 sets the overall bandwidth; you have to of course make sure that G2, G3 and CM2 have at least 2x higher bandwidth than the overall amplifier to make sure that you have enough loop=gain for this CM1, and of course the load capacitance will add an additional pole

One thing to point out might be an obvious point, you always need to make sure that each Miller compensation is applied across a negative and overall negative gain stage as is shown here G2 is positive, G3 is negative, overall the amplifier has a negative gain; therefore, Miller compensation will work.

Slide 56 –Feed-forward Compensation
Another technique for three-stage amplifiers is to add a feed-forward capacitance across a positive gain stage; this reduces the delay at high frequencies, and therefore, is commonly used in bipolar amplifiers then need an impedance stage to drive the base current for the output stage.

This is quite interesting to take a closer look at, as the feedback element is across the positive gain stage and it creates a negative impedance, it can also introduce distortion effects due to AC coupling between the output and input nodes. The feed-forward capacitance and the parasitics also form a capacitive divider that reduces the overall gain of the output stage.

Slide 58 –Multipath NM (Feed-forward) Compensation [3,4]
I said that two-stage amplifiers provide the best dynamic response and power-to-bandwidth ratio, but have lower gain and therefore higher DC errors; three stages provide of course more gain, lower distortion, but at the expense of bandwidth and dynamic response. So is there a way to combine all of these advantages, into one architecture? And this is what multipath Nested Miller compensation is shown here is set out to do.

The low frequency behavior is now determined by the series high gain path formed by the series combination of G1, G2 and G3; this enables a low offset and good distortion.

At high frequencies the stages G1and G2 are bypassed by the parallel gate stage GFF and the amplifier behaves as a two-stage amplifier set by GFF, the Miller compensation and G3.

This in the frequency response, this feed-forward path effectively introduces a left half-plane zero that cancels out one of the poles that are formed by the three-stage architecture. Potential pitfalls can be a mismatch of this pole-zero doublet, again leading to settling tails, and of course also that during slewing, it is possible that the feed-forward stage gets overwhelmed; therefore, we do lose one of the key part of our compensation and amplifier can become unstable.

Slide 59 –Multipath Nested Miller Compensation
The arrangement is really great for power efficiency, which I will show in the next slide; the feed-forward path introduces a zero which is intended to cancel out one of the poles of the three-stage paths; to get an exact cancellation, the transconductance to Miller capacitances should be equal.

If this is not the case, a pole-zero doublet is formed; this shows up as a discontinuity in a magnitude and a phase plot, and right around the frequency where the feed-forward path is supposed to take over; we call this particular frequency, the stitching frequency.

If the mismatch is large or happens at a frequency much lower than the bandwidth, then it will create very slow settling tails in the transient response, and leading to some system level concerns. The feed-forward stage also introduces complex output impedance which can create issues when driving sampling networks and ADCs.
